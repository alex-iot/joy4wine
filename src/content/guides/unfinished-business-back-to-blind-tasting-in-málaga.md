Unfinished business: back to blind tasting in Málaga

[Photo: round one glasses with the tasting grid, as the lead image, or the reveal of the round 1 bottles]

Halfway through my WSET Diploma, we moved to Málaga. Something had to give, and it was D3, the Wines of the World exam and the hardest part of the Diploma. I was booked to sit it at the end of last year. I didn’t.

The first year here went on everything else: settling in as a family, work, getting to know a new place. Only now does it feel like there’s room for the unfinished business. I’ve restarted my studies part-time, and the plan is to sit the exam next May.

Theory you can pick up again with books and a routine. Tasting is different. It runs on rhythm, and a year away had broken mine.

Finding the right table

That’s how I ended up at the Iberian Wine Academy on Calle Eslava. It’s run by Jonas Tofterup, one of only a handful of Masters of Wine in Spain. Alongside the WSET courses, he runs advanced blind tastings for students preparing for the Diploma and Master of Wine exams.

The session I joined on 12 October was the twelfth. Sixteen of us sat around the table, some from as far away as Granada and Córdoba. Having something like this so close to home is a piece of luck I don’t take for granted.

[Photo: selfie in the tasting room. Caption: Sixteen of us, 24 wines, one long morning.]

How it works

The format mirrors the exams. There are twenty-four wines, served in two rounds of twelve. Before each round you’re told what links the wines in each flight. Then you taste on your own, in silence, with about three and a half minutes per wine to look, smell, taste, write and decide. Afterwards, Jonas leads a group discussion of each wine. For every glass you’re expected to work out the grape or grapes, the vintage and the quality level, and where it comes from.

It’s a busy session, and it doesn’t let up.

[Photo: glasses 1–12 with the grid. Caption: Round one: twelve glasses, one grid, and the clock running.]

What was in the glass

The first round was five whites, four fortified wines and three sparkling. The whites were all Chardonnay, and together they showed the point of the exercise well. They ranged from a €9 Pays d’Oc to an €85 Chassagne-Montrachet Premier Cru and a €120 Napa Valley, with Yarra Valley and Elgin in between. It was the same grape making very different wines, and that price spread is exactly what you’re meant to pick up blind. The fortified wines covered Sherry, Madeira and white Port. The sparkling flight was all Champagne, topped by a 2012 Taittinger Comtes de Champagne.

[Photo: round 1 bottles. Caption: The reveal, round one: five Chardonnays, four fortified wines and three Champagnes.]

The second round was built differently: three wines from a single country, three classic European origins, and then six with nothing connecting them at all. That last group was a mixed bag, running from a Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc to a Barolo and a 20-year-old tawny Port, and it’s the hardest kind of flight to taste blind, because there’s no clue to lean on.

[Photo: round 2 bottles. Caption: Round two, from Châteauneuf-du-Pape Blanc to a 20-year-old tawny.]

What I took away

As a refresher, it was exactly what I needed. Jonas shared some good tips and mental models for blind tasting. With the Champagnes, for example, the first question isn’t the house or the grape but the tier: is this a non-vintage, a vintage or a prestige cuvée? Place the wine in its quality level first, and the rest of the deduction has something to build on. It also reminded me, quite bluntly, how much work I still have to do before the tasting exams.

[Photo: Jonas pouring at the whiteboard. Caption: Jonas working through the Champagne flight: non-vintage, vintage or prestige cuvée?]

Most of all, it whetted my appetite for more, and gave me the motivation I needed to get back into my studies properly.
